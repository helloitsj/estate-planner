New York · Guide 02

A will's guardian doesn't operate during incapacity

Published 2026-07-23 · Updated 2026-07-24 · New York

Educational information, not legal advice. This guide describes how New York instruments generally operate. It does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

The guardian you name in your will takes effect only when you die. If you and your child's other parent are alive but unable to parent — both hospitalized after the same accident, say — that will-named guardian has no authority, because nobody has died and the will has not activated. New York addresses this living gap with a separate instrument: a standby guardian designation under SCPA 1726, which can operate on a parent's incapacity rather than on death.

The confusion is understandable. A will's guardian nomination feels like it says "this is who raises our kids if something happens to us." But a will only speaks at death. "Something happening" also includes the state where both parents are alive but neither can care for a child — and a will is silent there.

Two different moments, two different instruments

Guardianship of a child can be needed at two very different times: after both parents have died, and while both parents are alive but unable to care for the child. New York uses a different tool for each, and a plan that has one but not the other has a real hole in it.

  • A will's guardian nomination operates at death. It is the right tool for the both-parents-gone case.
  • A standby guardian designation (SCPA 1726) can be written to operate on incapacity, giving a named person clean legal authority to step in while the parents are alive but unable to act.

What the will's guardian nomination actually does

A guardian named in a will is a nomination. It tells the Surrogate's Court whom the parents wanted, and courts give that choice great weight — but it operates only once the will does, which is at death. Until then it sits dormant, along with the rest of the will. That is exactly the right design for the case it's built for: both parents gone, and someone needed to raise the children. It simply doesn't reach the living-but-incapacitated case, because the will hasn't spoken.

The standby guardian designation (SCPA 1726)

New York's Surrogate's Court Procedure Act § 1726 lets a parent name a standby guardian in advance, whose authority begins on a defined triggering event rather than at death. The triggers the statute recognizes include the parent's incapacity, the parent's debilitation (with the parent's written consent), an administrative separation such as immigration detention, and death. That first trigger — incapacity — is what covers the gap a will can't reach.

Two features of the designation are worth understanding:

  • It creates concurrent authority. When it takes effect on incapacity, it does not strip the parent of their rights; the standby guardian and the parent hold authority side by side. That is what makes it a loan of authority rather than a transfer — it can be set up so that when a parent recovers, they simply resume, without a court having to hand anything back.
  • It is time-limited unless followed up. Once a standby guardian's authority commences, a formal guardianship petition generally has to be filed within about 60 days, or the authority lapses. The designation is a bridge to a court-supervised arrangement, not a permanent substitute for one.

How the two fit together

The will's guardian and the standby guardian are usually the same people, named in the same order — the difference is only when each can act. Keeping the two documents aligned is the thing to watch: if the will names one set of people and a standby designation names another, the plan points in two directions at the worst possible moment.

Most acute for a single parent

For a married or partnered couple, if one parent is incapacitated the other usually keeps parenting, and the standby designation matters mainly for the rarer both-incapacitated case. For a single parent, there is no second parent to fall back on. If the only parent is alive but unable to care for the child even briefly, the will — which speaks only at death — offers nothing, and without a standby designation no one holds clean legal authority until a court acts. The gap the standby designation fills is the same in either household; it's simply more exposed when there's one parent rather than two.

Worth taking to your attorney

Phrased as questions, not conclusions. Does my plan cover who parents my children if I am — or my co-parent and I are — alive but incapacitated, not only if we die? Would a standby guardian designation under SCPA 1726 fit our situation, and would it name the same people, in the same order, as the guardian in the will?
