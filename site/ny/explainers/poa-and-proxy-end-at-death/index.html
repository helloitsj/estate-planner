New York · Guide 01

A power of attorney dies when you do

Published 2026-07-23 · New York

Educational information, not legal advice. This guide describes how New York instruments generally operate. It does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

A New York power of attorney and a health care proxy both let someone act for you while you are alive but unable to act for yourself — one for money and property, one for medical decisions. That authority ends the instant you die. It never survives death. At the moment of death a different instrument takes over: your will and the executor it names. An agent's authority and an executor's authority never overlap — one ends exactly where the other begins.

This trips people up because the documents sit in the same folder and get signed at the same meeting, so they feel like one continuous grant of "someone can handle things for me." They are not. They govern different windows of time:

  • A health care proxy and a power of attorney are live only while you are alive and (for most powers) unable to act. They switch off at death.
  • A will is the opposite: dormant your whole life, it activates only at death, when the court appoints the executor to wind up your affairs.

So there is no gap and no overlap — but there is a common misconception in each direction. Nobody can "use the power of attorney" to pay for the funeral after death; the power died with the person. And the executor has no say over medical care, because by the time an executor exists, there is no longer a patient.

Worth taking to your attorney

Ask your attorney: in the window between my death and my executor being formally appointed, who is authorized to make urgent decisions — and does my plan name that person anywhere? It's a question about a real gap, not a conclusion about your plan.
