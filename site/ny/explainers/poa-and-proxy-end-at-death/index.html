New York · Guide 01

A power of attorney dies when you do

Published 2026-07-23 · Updated 2026-07-24 · New York

Educational information, not legal advice. This guide describes how New York instruments generally operate. It does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

A New York power of attorney and a health care proxy both let someone act for you while you are alive but unable to act for yourself — one for money and property, one for medical decisions. That authority ends the instant you die. It never survives death. At the moment of death a different instrument takes over: your will and the executor it names. An agent's authority and an executor's authority never overlap — one ends exactly where the other begins.

This trips people up because the documents sit in the same folder and get signed at the same meeting, so they feel like one continuous grant of "someone can handle things for me." They are not. They govern different windows of time, and knowing where one ends and the next begins is most of what there is to understand here.

The two windows of time

Think of a single line running through your life. For the stretch when you are alive but cannot act for yourself — unconscious, hospitalized, or otherwise unable to decide — two instruments are live. For the stretch after death, a different one is. They meet at the moment of death and hand off; they do not run at the same time.

  • A power of attorney and a health care proxy operate only while you are alive. They switch off at death.
  • A will is dormant your entire life. It activates only at death, when the court appoints the executor to wind up your affairs.

What a power of attorney does while you're alive

A power of attorney authorizes someone — your agent — to handle financial and property matters for you: paying bills, managing accounts, dealing with the landlord or the bank. In New York this is usually the statutory short-form power of attorney. Two features matter for the timing:

  • It is typically durable, meaning it keeps working after you lose capacity. That is the whole point — a power that stopped the moment you couldn't act would be useless in exactly the situation you drafted it for.
  • It can name a successor agent to step in if your first choice can't serve.

What durability does not do is carry the authority past death. "Durable" means it survives your incapacity, not your death. The two are different events, and the power stops at the second one.

The health care proxy is the other half

A health care proxy names a health care agent to make medical decisions when you can't make them yourself. It covers the same alive-but-unable window, on the medical side. One New York particularity is worth knowing: the state has you name one agent at a time — you cannot appoint two people to serve together as co-agents. You name a first agent, then a successor behind them. Like the power of attorney, the proxy ends at death.

At death, the will takes over

When you die, the power of attorney and the health care proxy switch off, and the will switches on. The person named as executor asks the Surrogate's Court to be formally appointed; once appointed, the executor gathers the assets, pays debts and taxes, and distributes what remains under the will. The executor's job is entirely about the estate — property and money — and begins only once there is an estate to administer.

The gap people don't expect

There is a practical seam between death and the executor's formal appointment. For a short window, no one holds full legal authority over the estate, because the agent's power died with the person and the executor hasn't been appointed yet. Urgent, immediate decisions in that window — funeral and burial or cremation arrangements especially — aren't governed by the power of attorney at all. New York treats who controls the disposition of your remains as a separate designation, one that does operate after death, precisely because the financial power of attorney cannot. That's a distinct document from the three above, and an easy one to leave out.

Two misconceptions, one in each direction

Almost everyone gets one of these backwards:

  • You cannot "use the power of attorney" after death — for example, to reach an account to pay for the funeral. The power is void the moment the person dies.
  • The executor has no say over medical care — by the time an executor exists, there is no longer a patient to make decisions for.

Why this is worth keeping straight

Dollar thresholds change every year; these mechanics don't. "A power of attorney ends at death," "a health care proxy ends at death," "the will begins at death" are true regardless of the year or anyone's net worth. Get the sequence right once and it stays right.

Worth taking to your attorney

Two questions this raises, phrased as questions rather than conclusions. In the window between my death and my executor being formally appointed, who is authorized to make urgent decisions — and does my plan name that person anywhere? And separately: have I named who controls funeral and disposition decisions, given that my power of attorney can't reach that far?
