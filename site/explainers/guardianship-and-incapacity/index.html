New York · Guide 02

A will's guardian doesn't operate during incapacity

Published 2026-07-23 · New York

Educational information, not legal advice. This guide describes how New York instruments generally operate. It does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

The guardian you name in your will takes effect only when you die. If you and your child's other parent are alive but unable to parent — both hospitalized after the same accident, say — that will-named guardian has no authority, because nobody has died and the will has not activated. New York addresses this living gap with a separate instrument: a standby guardian designation under SCPA 1726, which can operate on a parent's incapacity rather than on death.

The confusion is understandable. A will's guardian nomination feels like it says "this is who raises our kids if something happens to us." But a will only speaks at death. "Something happening" also includes the state where both parents are alive but neither can care for a child — and a will is silent there.

  • A will's guardian nomination operates at death. It is the right tool for the both-parents-gone case.
  • A standby guardian designation (SCPA 1726) can be written to operate on incapacity, giving a named person clean legal authority to step in while the parents are alive but unable to act.

Drafted well, the standby designation is a loan of authority, not a transfer: it can be set to end the moment either parent recovers. Without it, if both parents are alive but incapacitated, no one holds clean legal authority over the children until a court acts.

Worth taking to your attorney

Ask your attorney: does my plan cover who parents my children if my co-parent and I are both alive but incapacitated — not only if we die? And would a standby guardian designation under SCPA 1726 fit our situation? These are questions to raise, not a verdict on your plan.
