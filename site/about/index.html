About this site

What this is, who writes it, how the numbers are checked, and — just as important — what it deliberately is not.

Educational information, not legal advice. This page, like every page here, describes how New York instruments generally operate. It is not a legal instrument, does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Nothing here creates an attorney–client relationship. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

Every estate-planning resource online is indexed by instrument — "what is a power of attorney." This one is indexed by life state: who decides what, and when. Nobody wonders what a power of attorney is. They wonder who signs for the apartment while they're unconscious, and whether that changes the moment they die.

What this is

A plain-language explanation of how New York estate-planning instruments behave across the arc of a life — while you're well, while you're incapacitated, and after death. It exists to help you walk into a meeting with a licensed attorney already understanding the mechanics, so that expensive hour is spent on your situation rather than on definitions.

Who writes it

The disclaimers here are not false modesty: this site describes how the law works in general terms; it never evaluates anyone's situation or tells anyone what to do. Where a decision belongs to you and your attorney, the site hands it to them as a question, never a conclusion.

How the numbers are checked

Dollar figures, thresholds, percentages, statutory citations, and deadlines are the easiest things to get wrong and the most consequential. So none are written from memory. Every number is fetched from its primary source — the IRS, the New York State Department of Taxation and Finance, and the New York Consolidated Laws — and recorded with the date it was checked. You can see all of them, with their last-verified dates, on the Sources page.

Wherever there's a choice, this site states the durable mechanism rather than the changeable number. "A power of attorney terminates at death" is true forever. New York's estate-tax exclusion changes every January. The former is both the more useful fact and the one incumbents skip.

What this is not

  • It does not generate legal documents.
  • It does not recommend, rate, or match attorneys.
  • It does not collect your email, create accounts, or take payment.
  • It has no analytics and no server. When you use the prepare tool, your answers are stored only in your own browser and are never transmitted anywhere.
  • It covers New York only. Other states differ, sometimes fundamentally.

Corrections

If a figure is stale or a mechanism is described wrongly, that's a defect worth fixing. The source for every number is listed on the Sources page with the date it was last verified.
