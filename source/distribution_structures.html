Children's Trusts · New York

How the kids receive it — six ways

Six structures side by side. Each controls when children receive money and how protected it is once they do. The trade-offs below are the ones that usually decide it.

Educational information, not legal advice. This page describes how New York instruments generally operate. It is not a legal instrument, does not account for any individual situation, and law varies by state and changes over time. Consult a licensed New York attorney.

The tax reality that applies across all six

  • Federal estate tax — ignore it. The exemption is $15M per person. A child would need an estate above that threshold at their own death for it to apply.
  • NY estate tax — the one that can bite, modestly. New York's exclusion is far lower than the federal one and carries a hard cliff. A child who inherits and grows it can cross that at their own death. See /sources for current figures.
  • The step-up trade-off drives the real decision. Money a child owns gets a basis step-up at their death (heirs skip capital-gains tax). Money locked in a protective lifetime trust usually does not — for a family under the New York exclusion, the lost step-up often costs more than the estate tax it saves. Lifetime trusts are a divorce/creditor shield, not a tax play.
1

Staggered outright — 25 / 30 / 35

Thirds vest outright at each age. The most common default.

Keeps step-up

✓ For

  • Simple, familiar, cheap to run
  • Autonomy arrives in stages — a mistake at 25 costs only a third
  • Assets get a basis step-up at the child's death

✕ Against

  • 25 is young to receive a third of a large sum
  • Once vested, fully exposed to the child's divorce, creditors, lawsuits
  • No protection at all after the final tranche

2

Later staggered — 30 / 35 / 40

Same shape, pushed later.

Keeps step-up

✓ For

  • Same simplicity, more maturity before large sums land
  • Still gets the step-up
  • 30 is a more common "first meaningful tranche" age than 25

✕ Against

  • Still fully exposed once each tranche vests
  • A child in their 20s waits longer for real access
Discuss  Does a first tranche at 30 rather than 25 fit better?
3

Single age — all at 30 (or 35)

One lump, one handoff.

Keeps step-up

✓ For

  • Simplest possible structure
  • Clean single transfer of control

✕ Against

  • All-or-nothing — one bad decision risks everything at once
  • No staging to let the child learn on a smaller slice first
  • Rarely advised unless the amount is small
Discuss  Generally only worth it when the trust is expected to be modest.
4

Lifetime / beneficiary-controlled trust

Never distributed outright. Child becomes co-trustee ~30, controlling trustee ~35 — but assets stay in trust for life.

Loses step-up*

✓ For

  • Strongest shield — protects from the child's divorce, creditors, lawsuits
  • Keeps the inheritance out of the child's own taxable estate
  • Child still controls it and can direct where it goes at death

✕ Against

  • Forfeits the basis step-up — real capital-gains cost on equity-heavy assets, usually outweighing any NY estate-tax saving
  • More complex; ongoing trust tax filings
  • Some children experience it as distrust
Discuss  Is protection from a future divorce or lawsuit worth the added complexity?  *A "step-up toggle" (trust-protector power) can restore the step-up when there's no estate-tax cost — best of both, more drafting.
5

Hybrid — partial outright + lifetime trust

A defined slice vests outright (house / business money); the rest stays in a protective lifetime trust.

Mixed

✓ For

  • Gives a real "this is mine" chunk plus lifetime protection on the balance
  • Balances autonomy against asset protection
  • The outright slice gets a step-up; the protected slice is shielded

✕ Against

  • More complex — two regimes in one document
  • The split has to be decided
Discuss  How much should be theirs free and clear versus protected for life? A common split is one tranche outright, remainder in trust.
6

Pot / family trust, then split

All the money stays in one common pool until the youngest reaches a set age (~23), then divides into equal shares.

Suits an age gap

✓ For

  • Built for a wide age gap — the trustee spends by need, like a parent, so a younger child's upbringing and college aren't shortchanged
  • Keeps funds flexible during the minority years
  • Then converts to any structure above for the split shares

✕ Against

  • The older child's share isn't fixed during the pot years — can feel unequal
  • Needs a clear "split age" and a downstream structure chosen
Discuss  With a wide age gap, is one common pot through the minority years, then a split, the better fit?

A common starting point

A frequently drafted shape: pot trust through the minority years (handles an age gap) → split into staggered shares at 30 / 35 / 40 → with an optional lifetime-trust wrapper plus step-up toggle where divorce or creditor protection matters. This is a pattern to react to, not a recommendation.

For most families this isn't primarily a tax decision — below the state exclusion the tax differences are second-order. It's a decision about autonomy versus protection and how to handle an age gap.

Questions to settle together

  1. Common pot during the minority years, or separate shares from day one?
  2. First meaningful tranche at 25, 30, or later?
  3. Full autonomy by the mid-30s, or lifetime protection with the child as trustee?
  4. If protection matters: full lifetime trust, or a hybrid with a chunk outright?
